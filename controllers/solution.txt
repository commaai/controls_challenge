 comma Controls Challenge v2 — Solution
 Controller: controllers/custom.py

1. THE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
"TinyPhysics" is a neural network that simulates a car's lateral (steering)
dynamics. A controller must output a steer command in [-2, 2] at 10 Hz so the
car's actual lateral acceleration tracks a target trajectory. Rollouts are
scored on two competing costs:

  lataccel_cost = mean((target - actual)^2) * 100      (tracking accuracy)
  jerk_cost     = mean((diff(actual) / 0.1)^2) * 100    (smoothness)
  total_cost    = lataccel_cost * 50 + jerk_cost

At every step the controller sees: the current target lateral acceleration,
the measured lateral acceleration, the car state (v_ego, a_ego, road-roll
lataccel), and a future plan (the next ~49 steps of targets/roll/speed).

2. APPROACH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
(a) System identification first. A step-response probe (hold steer at 0, then
    step it to 0.5 and watch the response) showed the simulated car reacts
    with ~1-2 control steps (0.1-0.2 s) of actuator lag. Any controller that
    reacts only to the instantaneous target is therefore permanently late —
    it pays twice, in tracking error and in the jerky corrections that lag
    causes.

(b) Design around the lag, not against it. Since the future plan is given,
    the controller tracks a "lookahead target": the mean of the current
    target and the next 4 planned targets. This centers the tracking point
    ~2 steps into the future (matching the measured lag) and simultaneously
    low-pass filters the target, which directly reduces jerk. This one idea
    was worth ~12% of total cost before any gain tuning.

(c) PID feedback does the rest, tuned by grid search (8 rounds, ~60 configs)
    on training segments, with tail statistics (median/p95/max) tracked —
    not just the mean — and final selection validated on 100 held-out
    segments never used during tuning.

3. HOW THE CONTROLLER WORKS (controllers/custom.py)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  # Lookahead target: mean of current target and the next 4 planned targets
  target = mean([target_lataccel] + future_plan.lataccel[:4])

  error           = target - current_lataccel
  error_integral += error            (clamped to [-10, 10], anti-windup)
  error_diff      = error - prev_error

  action = 0.18 * error + 0.13 * error_integral - 0.04 * error_diff

Why each piece exists:

  * Lookahead target — delay compensation + target smoothing (see 2b).

  * P (0.18) and I (0.13): the integral does the steady-state work (e.g.
    holding steer against road roll); the proportional term handles
    transients. Integral gains beyond ~0.16 hit an instability cliff where
    the loop oscillates and jerk cost explodes — 0.13 leaves margin.

  * D (-0.04): small negative derivative gain damps fast error changes and
    measurably lowers jerk in this noisy, laggy plant.

  * Integral clamp (±10) — the critical robustness parameter. The cost
    distribution is dominated by rare hard segments where the car slows to
    near standstill: steering loses authority, targets become unreachable,
    and the integral winds up. Unclamped, the wound-up integral causes a
    huge overshoot when authority returns; clamped too tight (2-5), the
    controller can't hold the large sustained steer hard segments need.
    Clamp = 10 cut the worst-segment cost ~5x with no loss elsewhere.

Ideas tested and rejected (made results worse or no better):
  * Explicit feedforward on planned target change (redundant with lookahead).
  * Low-pass smoothing of the steer action (added lag, net worse).
  * Longer lookahead (6-8 steps): over-anticipates, tracking degrades.
  * Tight integral clamps (2-5): starves hard segments of steer authority.

4. HOW TO VERIFY THE SCORE AND TEST OUTPUTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
(a) Single-segment sanity check with live debug plots:

  python tinyphysics.py --model_path ./models/tinyphysics.onnx \
      --data_path ./data/00000.csv --debug --controller custom

(b) Batch score over many segments:

  python tinyphysics.py --model_path ./models/tinyphysics.onnx \
      --data_path ./data --num_segs 100 --controller custom

  Prints average lataccel/jerk/total cost. Lower is better; total_cost < 100
  is the challenge's bar for a competitive score. Re-run with
  --controller pid to compare against the baseline.

(c) Official submission run (required for the leaderboard — 5000 segments):

  python eval.py --model_path ./models/tinyphysics.onnx \
      --data_path ./data --num_segs 5000 \
      --test_controller custom --baseline_controller pid

  This writes ./report.html. The report is ready when eval.py prints
  "Report saved to: './report.html'" and exits. To confirm the file is
  complete, check that it ends with a closing html tag:

      tail -c 20 report.html        # should show "</body></html>"

  Open report.html in a browser and verify it contains: the aggregate cost
  table (custom vs pid means), cost-distribution histograms, 5 sample
  rollout plots, and the green banner "Test Controller (custom) passed
  Baseline Controller (pid)".

Rollouts are deterministic per segment (the simulator seeds numpy from the
md5 of the data path), so repeated runs give identical costs.
